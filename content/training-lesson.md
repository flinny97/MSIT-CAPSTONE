# Training Lesson: How to Spot and Report Phishing

Phishing is a way of deceiving an individual into providing sensitive information to bad actors. For example, someone may send a fake message to trick you into clicking a link, opening a file, or giving away information. Phishing is the most reported type of cybercrime in the United States. In 2024, the FBI received 193,407 phishing and spoofing complaints, which was more than any other type of crime (Federal Bureau of Investigation [FBI], 2025). Most online attacks start with a single click (Cybersecurity and Infrastructure Security Agency [CISA], n.d.). The goal of this lesson is to help review a message before you act on it.

This lesson covers five warning signs and the steps to take when you see one. Each part relates to a question previously answered. The same warning signs come up again in the post-assessment.

## Part 1: Check Who Really Sent It

The name you see on an email may not always match the person who sent it. For example, someone can change their display name to "IT Support" to make the email appear legitimate. You should always review the full email address instead of only looking at the name. Real Harborline emails should come from addresses that end in @harborline.example. For example, an email signed "System Administrator" that comes from an outside company or a free email account could be a phishing attempt. Even an email with the correct address can be suspicious because attackers can sometimes spoof email addresses. Vague names like "Help Desk" or "Support Team" should also be verified if the sender is unfamiliar. If you are not sure who sent something, contact that person or team using contact information you already have instead of replying. Reviewing the full email address and verifying the sender can help prevent phishing attempts.

## Part 2: Do not get lost in URGENCY

Scammers use pressure and urgency as tools. A person who feels rushed or panicked may not stop to check. Watch for deadlines like "URGENT within 24 hours," threats that your account will be deleted, or requests to keep something secret. Another common trick is asking you not to call because the sender is in meetings or traveling. For example, a fake email from an executive may ask for a payment to a new bank account today and say there is no time to talk. The FBI reports that this type of scam, called business email compromise, caused over $2.7 billion in losses in 2024 (FBI, 2025). A real request can wait the few minutes it takes to check it. If a message makes you feel rushed, always stop and verify the source.

## Part 3: Check the Link Before You Click or Sign In

A link can say one thing but redirect you to a different website. Before any action, hover your mouse over the link and check the full address that appears (CISA, n.d.). The important action to remember is identifying the actual website domain instead of only looking at the company name. For example, login.harborline.example is the expected address in this training scenario, but login.harborline.example.account-check.example actually belongs to account-check.example. You should also watch for small changes like a zero in place of the letter "o" or extra words added to the company name. A padlock icon or the Harborline logo does not mean the sign-in page is legitimate. Microsoft found fake sign-in pages that copied company branding to make the page look more convincing (Microsoft Threat Intelligence, 2022). If you need to sign in, always use the company's approved login page instead of clicking on links from suspicious emails.

## Part 4: Never Share Passwords, Codes, or Bank Details

Harborline IT will never ask for your password or the code from your authenticator app. Attackers have called and messaged employees by pretending to be the help desk to obtain access to their accounts (CISA & FBI, 2023). The same applies to payroll and banking information. Criminals have sent phishing emails to employees to steal their login credentials and change where paychecks are being sent (FBI, 2018). If you need to update your direct deposit, open the HR portal yourself instead of using a link from an email. If your phone shows a sign-in approval that you did not request, deny it and report it immediately. You should only enter your password or authentication codes through Harborline's approved sign-in process and never share them with anyone.

## Part 5: Be Careful With Unexpected Attachments

An attachment you were not expecting is a risk, even when the email looks legitimate. Some phishing emails may say you have a new voicemail and attach an HTML file that leads to a fake sign-in page (Microsoft Threat Intelligence, 2022). Others name the file like an Excel invoice, but it opens a browser window that asks you to re-enter your password (Microsoft Threat Intelligence, 2021). A real file will not ask for your password to open it. Before you open an attachment, ask yourself if you expected it and if it came from the sender's real address.

## Part 6: Not Every Email Is Phishing

Being careful does not mean reporting everything. A false alarm takes time away from the IT team and can make people start ignoring real warnings. An email is most likely legitimate when it comes from a real @harborline.example address and matches something you expected. It also should not ask for passwords, payments, or personal information. For example, a maintenance notice from the service desk that has no links and asks for nothing is a normal email. A reminder with a deadline is also normal as long as it tells you to use the portal you already know. Good security means reporting the emails that are genuinely suspicious.

## What to Do: Stop, Verify, Report

- Stop. Do not click any links, open attachments, or reply to the message.
- Verify. Contact the sender using a phone number, Teams account, or company directory listing you already have. Do not use the contact information in the message (CISA, n.d.).
- Report. Use the Report Phishing button in Outlook or contact the IT help desk.

If you already clicked a link or entered your password, report it to IT right away and change your password (McGill University, 2023). Use a different device to change it if you can. Reporting promptly matters more than feeling embarrassed.

## Quick Checklist

- **Sender:** Does the full address end in @harborline.example?
- **Urgency:** Is the message rushing me or threatening me?
- **Link:** Does the real address match the company it claims to be from?
- **Request:** Is it asking for a password, a code, a payment, or bank details?
- **Attachment:** Did I expect this file from this person?

If any answer is a problem, stop and verify the message before you do anything else.
